# age-horizon

## Core principle

Asset allocation should be driven primarily by **the horizon and purpose of each dollar**, not age alone. A user may have:

- A near-term cash need, such as tuition, rent, a home purchase, or a planned vehicle.
- An intermediate goal, such as a career break or education expense.
- A long-term retirement or legacy goal.

The same person may appropriately hold cash for one goal, bonds for another, and a diversified stock-heavy portfolio for retirement. Asset allocation is the division among stocks, bonds, cash, and other assets; the appropriate mix changes with the time horizon, risk tolerance, liquidity needs, financial situation, and objectives. The SEC defines risk tolerance as both the willingness and ability to lose money for potentially higher returns. [Investor.gov: Asset Allocation and Diversification](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/getting-started/asset-allocation); [FINRA Rule 2111 Suitability FAQ](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/key-topics/suitability/faq)

Age is useful as a rough proxy for the remaining accumulation period, but it is not a recommendation by itself. Retirement age, health and longevity expectations, pension or Social Security income, job stability, debt, dependents, other assets, spending flexibility, and the timing of specific goals can matter more than chronological age.

## How horizon affects stocks, bonds, and cash

### Stocks

Stocks have substantial short-term volatility and can lose principal. They are generally used for long-horizon growth and inflation protection, but a long horizon does not guarantee a positive outcome. A person with decades before a retirement goal may have time to recover from market declines and may reasonably accept more stock exposure than someone who must spend the money soon.

### Bonds

High-quality bonds and bond funds can provide diversification, income, and a source of portfolio withdrawals without selling stocks after a decline. They still have interest-rate, credit, reinvestment, and inflation risks; a bond fund is not the same as cash and can lose value.

### Cash and cash equivalents

Cash, insured deposits, Treasury bills, and similar instruments prioritize liquidity and nominal stability over long-term growth. They are generally appropriate for emergency needs and goals with a short or uncertain horizon. The SEC states that investors saving for a goal within **five years or less** generally should not choose risky investments, because they may have to sell at a loss when the money is needed. [Investor.gov: Gauge Your Risk Tolerance](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/save-and-invest/gauge-your-risk-tolerance)

Cash can also be useful immediately before and during retirement, but holding too much for too long can reduce expected growth and purchasing-power protection. The right amount depends on spending, other income, and how flexible withdrawals can be.

## Goal-based allocation

A practical framework is to assign investments according to when the money will be spent:

1. **Immediate or short-term needs:** cash or cash-like assets; avoid relying on stocks.
2. **Intermediate goals:** a mix of cash and high-quality short- or intermediate-term bonds, with stock exposure only if the goal date is flexible.
3. **Long-term goals:** a diversified portfolio with enough stocks for growth, balanced against the investor’s capacity and willingness to tolerate losses.
4. **Retirement withdrawals:** treat the first several years of planned withdrawals as especially sensitive to market declines; combine liquid reserves, high-quality bonds, and stocks for longer-term growth.

The goal date should be reassessed when it changes. A “retirement portfolio” may still need to fund expenses for decades after retirement, while a house down payment may have a much shorter horizon even if the investor is young.

## Rules of thumb and their limitations

### SEC five-year rule

The SEC’s educational guidance is that risky investments are generally unsuitable for money needed within **five years or less**. This is a useful risk-control rule, not a guarantee or a precise cutoff. A person may need a conservative allocation even with a somewhat longer horizon if the date is inflexible, the loss would be devastating, or the person has low risk capacity. Conversely, a goal nominally within five years might tolerate some risk if the goal can be delayed or reduced. [Investor.gov: Gauge Your Risk Tolerance](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/save-and-invest/gauge-your-risk-tolerance)

### Target-date glide paths

Target-date funds provide a widely used age-and-horizon framework. Vanguard’s published glide path illustrates one provider’s approach:

- At age **20**, it describes an allocation of approximately **90% stocks**.
- Around age **40**, it begins gradually reducing stock exposure.
- Around age **60**, it adds short-term Treasury inflation-protected securities while reducing volatility.
- At age **65**, the cited glide path has approximately **50% equities**.
- At age **72**, its default allocation reaches approximately **30% stocks and 70% bonds**.

These figures are Vanguard’s design, not an industry standard or a recommendation for every investor. Target-date funds differ substantially in stock exposure, international allocation, bond quality, fees, and whether the glide path reaches its most conservative allocation at or after the target date. The target date is not a guarantee that the investor will have enough money or avoid losses. [Vanguard: Target-Date Fund Glide Path](https://workplace.vanguard.com/investment/strategies/tdf-glide-path.html)

When evaluating a target-date fund, check:

- Whether the fund is designed to be most conservative **at** retirement or **through** retirement.
- Its stock, bond, inflation-protected bond, and international percentages.
- Its fees and underlying fund expenses.
- Whether the allocation fits the investor’s other retirement accounts, pension, Social Security, and non-retirement goals.
- Whether the investor can tolerate the fund’s potential losses near the target date.

A target-date fund can simplify retirement investing, but it should not be used automatically for money needed for college, a home, or other goals with a different date.

### Age-based stock rules

Rules such as “110 minus age” or “100 minus age” are heuristics rather than financial laws. They may be used as a starting point for discussion, but they omit:

- The actual retirement date and expected retirement length.
- Pension, Social Security, annuity, or other guaranteed income.
- Debt and emergency reserves.
- The investor’s ability and willingness to tolerate losses.
- Other household assets and concentrated holdings.
- Whether the portfolio is still accumulating or is being withdrawn.
- The timing and flexibility of non-retirement goals.

An early retiree may need a substantial growth allocation for longevity even at an older chronological age. A person with a secure pension may have greater capacity for stocks than an otherwise similar person without one. A young investor saving for tuition or a down payment may need more cash than an older investor saving for a distant legacy goal.

### Retirement cash-reserve rule

Charles Schwab describes one approach for retirees: maintain approximately **one year of expenses** after other income sources in cash investments, plus **two to four years of expenses** in high-quality short-term bonds or bond funds. This is a planning example, not a universal safe amount. It may be excessive for someone with very stable guaranteed income and flexible spending, or insufficient for a person with high spending volatility, little guaranteed income, or a very conservative risk preference. Reserves also have inflation and opportunity costs. [Charles Schwab: What Is Sequence-of-Returns Risk?](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/timing-matters-understanding-sequence-returns-risk)

## Target-date glide paths

A glide path gradually changes the stock/bond mix as the target date approaches. The usual rationale is:

- Early accumulation: prioritize long-term growth and accept volatility.
- Approaching retirement: reduce dependence on a favorable market outcome immediately before withdrawals begin.
- Retirement: retain enough stocks to address inflation and longevity risk while maintaining liquid, lower-volatility assets for spending.

A glide path should be evaluated against the investor’s **total balance sheet**, not in isolation. For example, a person with a substantial inflation-adjusted pension may already have bond-like income and could need more growth assets; a person relying almost entirely on an investment portfolio may need more liquidity and downside protection.

A target-date fund also does not eliminate sequence-of-returns risk. Its allocation can fall substantially near or after the target date, and the fund’s glide path may not match the investor’s actual retirement date or withdrawal needs.

## Sequence-of-returns risk

Sequence-of-returns risk is the danger that poor returns occur early in the withdrawal period. If a retiree withdraws a fixed amount while the portfolio is falling, the retiree must sell more shares to raise the same cash. The portfolio then has fewer assets available to participate in a later recovery. Two portfolios can experience the same long-run average returns but have materially different outcomes if the order of returns differs.

The risk is greatest when:

- Withdrawals begin after a market decline.
- The withdrawal rate is high relative to the portfolio.
- Spending rises with inflation regardless of portfolio performance.
- The investor has little non-portfolio income.
- The investor is forced to sell stocks to fund essential spending.
- Retirement is long and the portfolio must continue growing.

Potential mitigations include:

- Maintaining a cash and short-term bond reserve for planned spending.
- Diversifying across stocks, high-quality bonds, and cash rather than relying on one asset.
- Rebalancing instead of abandoning the plan after a decline.
- Temporarily reducing discretionary spending or delaying large purchases after poor returns.
- Using guaranteed income sources for essential expenses where appropriate.
- Reviewing withdrawals, taxes, and account location with a qualified professional.

None of these methods guarantees success. A large reserve can reduce growth, bonds can lose value, and flexible spending may not be possible. [Charles Schwab: What Is Sequence-of-Returns Risk?](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/timing-matters-understanding-sequence-returns-risk)

## Life-stage guidance

### College students

A college student often has two very different horizons:

- Money needed for tuition, housing, transportation, emergencies, or near-term obligations should generally remain liquid and should not depend on stock-market performance.
- Money genuinely intended for retirement and unlikely to be needed for decades may be invested in a diversified, low-cost, growth-oriented portfolio, subject to risk tolerance and financial foundations.

A student should not equate youth with unlimited risk capacity. An unexpected tuition bill, family emergency, unstable income, or high-interest debt can make cash more important than a nominally long retirement horizon. A target-date retirement fund may be a simple diversified option for retirement savings, but it is not a substitute for a cash reserve or a plan for education expenses.

### Early-career investors

Early-career investors often have a long retirement horizon and many future contributions ahead. They may be able to tolerate more stock exposure than someone approaching retirement, particularly when they have stable income, manageable debt, adequate liquidity, and the psychological ability to remain invested through large declines.

The allocation should still reflect:

- The date of any near-term home, education, or family goal.
- Job and income stability.
- Emergency savings and insurance.
- Employer retirement-plan choices.
- Existing exposure to the employer or industry.
- Whether the investor expects to retire early, which lengthens the withdrawal horizon.

Automatic contributions and periodic rebalancing can help maintain the intended mix, but neither prevents losses.

### Mid-career investors

Mid-career investors often have several simultaneous horizons: retirement, children’s education, home expenses, and possible career changes. They should avoid applying one stock/bond percentage to every account without separating these goals.

Useful questions include:

- Which dollars are needed first?
- Is the retirement date fixed or flexible?
- How much future income is pension-like or otherwise dependable?
- Could a job loss or caregiving obligation require liquidity?
- Are assets concentrated in an employer’s stock or another single investment?

As retirement becomes closer, reducing risk for the portion needed soon can be sensible. That does not necessarily mean making the entire portfolio conservative: funds for later retirement years may still have a long horizon and need growth.

### Pre-retirees

Pre-retirees should translate the target retirement date into a spending and income plan. They should test whether the portfolio can support essential and discretionary spending under unfavorable market conditions, rather than relying only on an average-return assumption.

The allocation process should address:

- The planned retirement date and whether it can be delayed.
- Essential spending versus flexible spending.
- Pension and Social Security timing.
- Health-care and long-term-care uncertainty.
- The amount of liquid cash and high-quality bonds needed for early withdrawals.
- Tax consequences of selling assets from different account types.
- The effect of a market decline immediately before retirement.

A gradual glide path can reduce dependence on a strong market immediately before retirement, but moving entirely to cash may create inflation and longevity risks.

### Retirees

Retirement is not necessarily a short horizon. A retiree may need assets to fund many years of spending and to preserve purchasing power. A balanced portfolio commonly combines:

- Cash or cash equivalents for immediate liquidity.
- High-quality bonds for near- and intermediate-term withdrawals.
- Diversified stocks and other growth assets for later spending and inflation risk.

The allocation should be reviewed when spending, health, income, family responsibilities, or market conditions change. Retirees should distinguish essential expenses, which may be supported by predictable income, from discretionary expenses, which can be adjusted if markets are weak.

Traditional IRAs and most traditional retirement-plan accounts generally require minimum distributions beginning at age **73** under current IRS guidance; Roth IRAs and designated Roth accounts generally do not require lifetime RMDs for the original owner. RMD rules affect withdrawal and tax planning but do not dictate a particular stock/bond allocation. [IRS: Retirement Plan and IRA Required Minimum Distributions FAQs](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/retirement-plan-and-ira-required-minimum-distributions-faqs)

## Practical LLM guidance

When answering an age-and-horizon question:

1. Ask what the money is for and when it will be needed.
2. Separate near-term, intermediate, and long-term goals.
3. Ask whether the date is flexible.
4. Assess both risk tolerance and risk capacity.
5. Include emergency reserves, debt, pensions, Social Security, insurance, and other assets in the context.
6. Discuss stocks, bonds, and cash as complementary tools rather than inherently good or bad choices.
7. Explain that target-date allocations are fund-specific and not guaranteed.
8. Warn that early retirement withdrawals create sequence-of-returns risk.
9. Avoid presenting “age minus” formulas as personalized advice.
10. Revisit the allocation after major changes in income, retirement timing, health, family obligations, or goal dates.

This is general educational information, not individualized investment, tax, or retirement advice.
