# Portfolio construction: low-cost index funds and ETFs

## Core principles

This is educational guidance, not an individualized allocation or a guarantee of returns. Start with the job the portfolio must do: identify each goal, its spending date, required liquidity, tax location, and the investor's ability and willingness to tolerate losses. Money needed soon, an emergency reserve, and high-interest debt should not be treated as long-horizon stock capital. Asset allocation (stocks, bonds, inflation-linked bonds, and cash) should follow the goal's horizon and risk capacity rather than a market forecast. A portfolio can lose money, and neither a mutual fund nor an ETF is FDIC-insured or guaranteed. [SEC, “Mutual Funds”](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/mutual-funds-and-exchange-traded-funds-etfs/mutual-funds); [SEC, “Asset Allocation, Diversification, and Rebalancing”](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/getting-started/investing-your-future/asset-allocation-diversification-and-rebalancing)

A robust default is a simple, strategic, low-turnover portfolio:

* **Domestic stocks:** a broad, low-cost total-market index fund or ETF rather than a collection of favored companies. It supplies exposure across U.S. company sizes and sectors, but it is still all-equity risk.
* **International stocks:** a broad ex-U.S. fund, or a global fund that includes both U.S. and non-U.S. shares. International exposure reduces dependence on one country's economy, currency, valuation, and policy, while introducing currency, political, regulatory, and market risks. There is no evidence-based universal U.S./international percentage for every person; document the chosen policy and avoid changing it merely because one region recently outperformed.
* **Investment-grade bonds:** a diversified bond fund matched to the job of the bond allocation. Bond funds still have interest-rate, credit, inflation, and (for non-U.S. bonds) currency risk; a bond fund is not the same as a bank deposit or a bond held to maturity.
* **TIPS:** Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities can help hedge U.S. inflation risk. TreasuryDirect says that their principal is adjusted with the Consumer Price Index, interest is paid on the adjusted principal, and the adjustment can affect federal tax even before maturity. A TIPS fund has additional price and duration volatility and does not guarantee that an investor's personal cost of living is fully hedged. [TreasuryDirect, “TIPS”](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/tips/); [IRS Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550)
* **Cash:** a reserve for emergencies, near-term spending, and known withdrawals. Use an appropriate insured deposit or Treasury/cash vehicle and consider yield, liquidity, credit/insurance status, and taxes. Cash reduces short-term volatility but has reinvestment and purchasing-power risk; it is not a substitute for the long-term growth allocation.

A three-fund-style structure (total domestic stock, total international stock, and total bond funds) is a useful simplicity benchmark, not a required prescription. TIPS and cash can be added when inflation sensitivity or liquidity is an explicit goal. Each additional fund should have a job; otherwise it often adds overlap and decision complexity rather than diversification. [Bogleheads, “Three-fund portfolio”](https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Three-fund_portfolio)

Prefer broad, transparent index funds/ETFs with a clear benchmark, appropriate index coverage, low total cost, adequate liquidity, and small tracking difference. “Index” does not automatically mean safe or cheap: a narrow sector, country, thematic, leveraged, inverse, or high-turnover index can be concentrated and costly. Passive management generally means less trading and often lower fees, but compare the actual fund and its benchmark. [SEC, “Mutual Funds”](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/mutual-funds-and-exchange-traded-funds-etfs/mutual-funds)

## Diversification and concentration policy

Diversification should be assessed at the look-through portfolio level: asset class, country, currency, company, sector, issuer, credit quality, and bond maturity/duration. A fund with many holdings diversifies company-specific risk, but it does not diversify a portfolio that is already dominated by stocks, one country, one sector, or one employer. Two funds can contain the same companies; adding both may mainly increase the same exposure. Aggregate employer stock, restricted stock, options, sector funds, and individual shares rather than judging each account separately. Include human capital, pension exposure, and real estate when considering concentration, even though those are not shown in a brokerage holdings list.

There is no regulator-mandated percentage that makes a portfolio “diversified,” and a limit is not a guarantee against loss. A practical educational guardrail is to keep any single self-selected company at no more than **5%** of investable financial assets and all self-selected company shares at no more than **10%**, with a stricter limit for an employer or otherwise highly correlated exposure. These are conservative policy defaults, not laws or a universal research optimum; broad funds may themselves have a larger position in a very large company. If an app uses these limits, label them as user-adjustable risk controls and explain that tax, compensation, and liquidity constraints can require a staged reduction. [FINRA, concentration-risk investor guidance](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/concentration-risk)

A “core/satellite” approach can accommodate a small individual-stock or thematic sleeve while keeping the broad index core responsible for most market exposure. Do not infer that a long list of tickers is diversified: check weights, common holdings, sectors, geography, and factor tilts. A single stock has idiosyncratic failure risk that a diversified fund is specifically designed to reduce. [SEC, “Mutual Funds”](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/mutual-funds-and-exchange-traded-funds-etfs/mutual-funds)

## Rebalancing

First write target weights and an allowed drift band. Rebalancing restores risk exposure; it is not a reliable return-enhancement strategy and should not be a reaction to headlines. Use new contributions, distributions, and withdrawals to move toward underweight assets before selling. In taxable accounts, consider capital gains, loss harvesting, wash-sale rules, bid-ask spreads, and taxes; tax-advantaged accounts generally make trades simpler, but account rules still apply.

A reasonable process is a review at least annually and after major changes in goals, income, liabilities, or risk capacity, with trades only when a holding crosses a precommitted band. A commonly used illustration is a **5-percentage-point** absolute drift band or a scheduled review every **6–12 months**; these are process examples, not evidence that those exact thresholds are optimal. [SEC, “Asset Allocation, Diversification, and Rebalancing”](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/getting-started/investing-your-future/asset-allocation-diversification-and-rebalancing)

## Evaluating fees and implementation

The expense ratio is the fund's annual operating expenses as a percentage of average assets, deducted from fund assets rather than normally billed as a separate invoice. Compare like with like: index scope, asset class, share class, services, tax treatment, liquidity, and tracking difference. Review the prospectus fee table for management, administration, acquired-fund expenses, waivers and their expiration, and any purchase, redemption, account, or distribution fees. For ETFs also consider the bid-ask spread, brokerage commission if any, premium/discount to NAV, creation/redemption mechanics, and trading costs. A low expense ratio can still produce poor investor results if the fund is illiquid or tracks its index poorly; a fund's total return is net of its operating expenses. [SEC, “Mutual Funds”](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/mutual-funds-and-exchange-traded-funds-etfs/mutual-funds); [FINRA Fund Analyzer](https://www.finra.org/investors/tools-and-calculators/finra-fund-analyzer)

For a stock, an expense ratio is not applicable: a displayed zero does not mean zero cost. Consider bid-ask spread, commissions, account fees, taxes, research/advisory costs, and the opportunity cost of uncompensated idiosyncratic risk. Compare the same benchmark and holding period, and use the fund's prospectus and shareholder report rather than relying on a marketing label.

## Dollar-cost averaging versus lump-sum investing

Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) invests a fixed amount on a schedule regardless of price. It can reduce the emotional and regret risk of committing everything immediately, but money held in cash has less market exposure and may earn less than the chosen portfolio. When a person already has investable cash and a suitable long horizon, lump-sum investing gives the portfolio market exposure sooner and has the higher expected return when the market's expected risk premium is positive; it does not avoid the possibility of a loss immediately after investing. Vanguard's research describes this trade-off and reports that DCA can be a behavioral solution even though delaying investment creates cash drag. [Vanguard, “Cost averaging: Invest now or temporarily hold your cash?”](https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/dam/corp/research/pdf/cost_averaging_invest_now_or_temporarily_hold_your_cash.pdf); [Vanguard, “How to invest a lump sum of money”](https://investor.vanguard.com/investor-resources-education/online-trading/dollar-cost-averaging-vs-lump-sum)

Therefore: invest promptly when the cash is truly long-term capital and the investor can tolerate the risk; use a short, predetermined DCA schedule when it prevents panic selling or abandonment of the plan; do not call ordinary investing from each paycheck DCA of an existing lump sum; and reserve taxes, emergency cash, and near-term spending before either choice. DCA is not a market-timing method and cannot make a risky allocation safe.

## Interpreting MCP lookup fields

Treat every value as a dated observation with a provider definition, benchmark, currency, and methodology—not as a timeless fact or a recommendation. Ask the MCP provider for the field's as-of date, source, whether it is trailing or forward, and whether it is security-level, weighted portfolio-level, or calculated by the application.

| Field | Meaning and proper use | Common traps; individual stock versus diversified fund |
|---|---|---|
| **Expense ratio** | Annual operating expenses divided by average fund assets, usually shown as a percentage. It is most meaningful for a mutual fund or ETF and should be compared with tracking difference and all-in trading costs. | Not applicable to an individual operating company. “0%” for a stock is not a free-investing claim. For a fund, distinguish gross from net expense ratio and check waiver terms; do not add the same fund's ratio repeatedly merely because it appears in multiple accounts. [SEC](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/mutual-funds-and-exchange-traded-funds-etfs/mutual-funds) |
| **P/E** | Price divided by earnings per share; it can be trailing or forward. It is a relative valuation descriptor, not a forecast of return or a measure of risk by itself. | For a stock, negative or zero earnings can make P/E meaningless; compare companies with similar business models and accounting conventions. For a fund, a reported P/E is generally a holding-weighted portfolio statistic and may use provider-specific treatment for negative earnings; it is not the P/E of one company and can change as holdings and prices change. |
| **Dividend yield** | Usually annualized dividends or distributions divided by current price/NAV, but the provider may use trailing distributions, a forward estimate, or SEC yield for a bond fund. | For a stock, a high yield can reflect a falling price and the dividend can be cut; it is not total return. For a fund, distributions reflect the portfolio and may include changing dividends, interest, and realized gains depending on the definition. Reinvested distributions still matter to total return. |
| **Beta** | A historical sensitivity estimate relative to a stated benchmark over a stated lookback period; a value near 1 means similar historical market sensitivity, not a promise. | Stock beta does not capture all downside, gap, liquidity, or company-failure risk. Fund beta is a portfolio statistic relative to its selected benchmark and depends on holdings, period, and calculation; it is not comparable across providers without those details. It is often not a useful primary descriptor for cash or a bond fund. |
| **Holdings** | The securities held, ideally with weights, asset type, sector/country, and holdings date. Holdings explain what the fund actually owns and allow look-through concentration checks. | A stock lookup has one issuer; a diversified fund may have many holdings, but the count alone does not show diversification. Holdings can change and may be stale, partial, aggregated, or delayed. Check top weights, sectors, countries, and whether derivatives or cash are included. |
| **Overlap** | A comparison of common holdings between two portfolios; “overlap” may mean shared names, shared market value, or weighted exposure. The formula is not universal. | A high overlap means the second fund may add less diversification than its name suggests; it does not mean the funds have identical risk, because weights, sectors, currencies, and bonds can differ. For individual stocks, overlap is usually either one issuer in common or not applicable; compare the stock against each fund's look-through holdings. |
| **Data timestamp / as-of date** | The date and time at which the provider says a price, holding, or calculated metric was observed. It is essential for judging freshness and comparing fields. | A current market price can coexist with a fundamentals figure from an earlier filing period and holdings from an earlier portfolio date. Do not combine a stale P/E or yield with a current price as if all were simultaneous. Display source, timezone, market status, and field-level timestamps; if absent, say the value may be stale and avoid precise conclusions. |

For MCP answers, distinguish a **stock** from a **fund** before interpreting fields. A stock's P/E, yield, and beta describe one issuer; it has no fund expense ratio and no diversification beyond the company's own operations. A fund's P/E, yield, beta, and holdings are aggregates or provider calculations, while its expense ratio is a direct fund cost. A fund can be diversified while still carrying substantial market, country, sector, duration, or top-holding exposure. Neither set of metrics, alone or out of date, determines suitability.
