## Risk goals and behavior

### Core principle

Portfolio risk should be determined by the interaction of:

- **Risk tolerance:** willingness to tolerate losses and volatility.
- **Risk capacity:** objective ability to absorb losses without jeopardizing essential needs or a goal.
- **Knowledge and experience:** ability to understand products, risks, fees, taxes, and account rules.
- **Goal characteristics:** amount needed, priority, flexibility, and when the money will be used.

These are not interchangeable. Someone may be emotionally comfortable with risk but unable to take it because a large loss would delay a home purchase. Conversely, someone with substantial financial resources may have high capacity but dislike volatility and therefore choose a more conservative portfolio. The SEC describes asset allocation as dependent on both time horizon and risk tolerance, while CFP Board’s planning framework also considers goals and the client’s wider financial and personal circumstances.  
Sources: [SEC Investor.gov—Asset Allocation and Diversification](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/getting-started/asset-allocation), [CFP Board—Code and Standards](https://www.cfp.net/ethics/code-of-ethics-and-standards-of-conduct)

### Risk tolerance

Risk tolerance is a person’s willingness to lose some or all of an investment in exchange for potentially greater returns. It is partly psychological and can change after a market decline, job loss, illness, divorce, or other life event. A questionnaire is only a starting point; the AI should test the result with plain-language scenarios:

- “If this portfolio temporarily fell substantially, could you continue contributing?”
- “Would you sell because the loss threatened a required goal, or because the decline was uncomfortable?”
- “How much loss would make you abandon the plan?”

The answer should not be treated as a promise that the user will behave rationally in a crisis. A user who claims to have high tolerance but says they would sell during a severe decline should generally be shown a less aggressive allocation or a smaller risky allocation.

Risk tolerance should influence the upper bound of portfolio risk, but it should not override a short deadline or an inability to withstand losses.

### Risk capacity

Risk capacity is the financial ability to accept investment losses. Assess it separately from preferences. Relevant factors include:

- stability and predictability of income;
- emergency savings and insurance;
- high-interest debt and other liabilities;
- dependents and essential spending;
- pension or other reliable lifetime income;
- job concentration in a particular company or industry;
- liquidity needs;
- flexibility to postpone or reduce a goal;
- whether a market loss would force borrowing or a sale at a bad time.

A young person with decades until retirement may have a long horizon but little capacity if they have no emergency reserve, unstable income, or imminent tuition needs. A household with a pension, secure income, and flexible spending may have more capacity, but still may have low tolerance. Capacity is also goal-specific: the same person can have high capacity for retirement assets and low capacity for a house deposit due soon.

The AI should use the **lower of tolerance and capacity** as a practical constraint. It should not imply that a high expected return compensates for a risk the user cannot financially survive.

### Knowledge and experience

Knowledge affects the suitability of implementation, not the user’s underlying capacity. A beginner may have a high tolerance for risk but still misunderstand:

- that a stock can lose a large portion of its value;
- the difference between a diversified fund and a narrow sector fund;
- the effects of fees, taxes, leverage, and liquidity;
- how bonds can also decline in value;
- the difference between historical and guaranteed returns.

For inexperienced users, the default educational path should favor simple, diversified, transparent investments and an allocation the user can explain. The AI should avoid encouraging options, margin, leveraged products, short-term trading, crypto speculation, or concentrated individual-stock positions merely because the user is enthusiastic. More knowledge does not eliminate market risk, and confidence should not be treated as evidence of competence.

The SEC notes that diversification can reduce the effect of one company or sector performing poorly, but a narrowly focused fund may not be diversified.  
Source: [SEC Investor.gov—Asset Allocation and Diversification](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/getting-started/asset-allocation)

## Match each goal to its own horizon

A household should not necessarily use one allocation for every objective. Create separate “buckets” or sub-portfolios for goals with different deadlines, then consider the total household exposure.

The relevant horizon is when the money may first be needed—not the user’s age. A long-term retirement account does not make money needed for a near-term house purchase long-term.

Vanguard describes a general framework in which goals within **1–3 years** may call for low risk, goals over **3–10 years** may use a diversified mix of stocks and bonds, and goals beyond **10 years** may have greater growth exposure while accepting more near-term risk. These are educational ranges, not rigid rules; the allocation must also reflect tolerance, capacity, goal flexibility, and the consequences of failure.  
Source: [Vanguard—Understanding Investment Goals and Goals-Based Investing](https://investor.vanguard.com/investor-resources-education/investing-goals)

### Home down payment

A down payment is usually a high-priority, date-sensitive goal. The portfolio should become more conservative as the purchase approaches, because a market decline shortly before closing could reduce the available deposit or force the buyer to delay the purchase.

- For a near-term purchase, prioritize principal stability and liquidity, such as an insured savings product, money-market deposit, short-term Treasury securities, or appropriately timed CDs. The AI should explain that investment products differ in insurance, liquidity, and price risk.
- For a distant or flexible purchase, a diversified portfolio may include some growth assets, but the user should define the maximum acceptable shortfall and gradually move the required amount toward safer holdings.
- Keep the emergency reserve separate from the down-payment fund.
- Do not count an uncertain market return as a required part of the purchase plan.
- Include closing costs, moving expenses, taxes, repairs, and a post-closing cash reserve in the planning discussion rather than treating the entire account balance as available for the deposit.

Vanguard specifically identifies a house down payment as a medium-term objective and describes a more conservative income-oriented approach for a short-term down-payment goal.  
Source: [Vanguard—Investing Goals](https://investor.vanguard.com/investor-resources-education/investing-goals), [Vanguard—Asset Allocation Models](https://investor.vanguard.com/investor-resources-education/education/model-portfolio-allocation)

### Education

Education funding should be separated by the expected enrollment date for each beneficiary. A child starting school soon has a different appropriate allocation from a newborn, even if both goals are labeled “college.”

- Match the investment horizon to the likely first withdrawal and later withdrawals.
- Reduce equity exposure as enrollment nears and preserve amounts needed for upcoming tuition and expenses.
- Consider a 529 plan or another appropriate account, but explain that tax treatment, qualified-expense rules, investment choices, beneficiary changes, and state benefits require current account-specific and tax information.
- Discuss uncertainty: education costs, scholarships, family contributions, school choice, and the possibility that the money may be needed earlier or later.
- Do not place essential near-term tuition money in a portfolio whose value could fall sharply.

The AI should distinguish the funding goal from the account wrapper. A 529 may be useful, but the account does not make a risky allocation safe. The SEC identifies education as a long-term goal when the funds will not be needed for many years, while the asset mix should change as the withdrawal date approaches.  
Source: [SEC Investor.gov—Introduction to Investing](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing)

### Early retirement or FIRE

FIRE combines a long accumulation period with an earlier and potentially longer withdrawal period. The user should not be guided solely by an aggressive accumulation allocation. The plan should address:

- the desired retirement date and spending level;
- flexibility to reduce spending or continue working;
- health insurance and other pre-Medicare costs;
- taxes and account-access restrictions;
- cash-flow needs before traditional retirement-account access;
- the effect of inflation and longevity;
- a reserve for several years of planned spending;
- a withdrawal and rebalancing policy;
- sequence-of-returns risk, especially when withdrawals begin during a market decline.

A long horizon can support meaningful growth exposure, but the risk of an early-retirement plan is not merely a temporary account decline. A large decline near the planned retirement date, or early in withdrawals, can permanently impair the plan if the household sells risky assets to fund spending. The AI should model adverse scenarios rather than promise a safe withdrawal rate or a particular retirement date.

FIRE users may also have unusually high tolerance for delayed consumption but low capacity once employment ends. The AI should reassess capacity when the user transitions from earning and contributing to withdrawing and relying on the portfolio.

### Long-term wealth building

For a flexible, multi-decade wealth goal, a diversified growth-oriented allocation may be reasonable for a user with sufficient capacity and tolerance. The educational sequence should be:

1. Control high-interest debt and create a cash reserve.
2. Capture any available employer retirement-plan match.
3. Use tax-advantaged accounts when appropriate, subject to current rules and eligibility.
4. Automate contributions and increase them when income rises.
5. Use broad, diversified, low-cost investments rather than relying on a few stocks.
6. Rebalance infrequently according to a stated policy.
7. Review the plan when goals, income, family circumstances, or risk capacity change.

The SEC emphasizes emergency savings, regular investing, employer plans, diversification, and long-term discipline as wealth-building practices. It also warns that frequent trading may be harmful over the long term.  
Source: [SEC Investor.gov—Build Wealth Over Time](https://www.investor.gov/build-wealth-over-time-through-saving-and-investing)

## Behavioral mistakes

### Panic selling

Panic selling occurs when a user abandons a long-term allocation during a market decline because the immediate emotional pain overwhelms the original plan. It can:

- turn a temporary decline into a realized loss;
- leave the portfolio too conservative to meet a long-term goal;
- cause the user to miss part of a subsequent recovery;
- create tax and transaction consequences.

The response should not shame the user or insist that they “just stay invested.” First determine whether the allocation was too risky, whether the goal has changed, or whether the user needs liquidity. If the goal and capacity remain unchanged, explain the difference between market volatility and permanent impairment, revisit the written allocation, and use automatic contributions or scheduled rebalancing to reduce emotional decisions.

### Return chasing

Return chasing means buying assets after strong recent performance because they appear certain to continue rising, or switching out of poorly performing assets without a change in the plan. It can produce concentration, high turnover, poor diversification, and buying at inflated prices. The AI should distinguish a justified allocation change—such as a changed horizon or capacity—from a performance-driven reaction.

Use total-portfolio exposure, valuation uncertainty, diversification, fees, taxes, and a predefined rebalancing rule rather than recent returns as the decision framework. Historical performance is not a guarantee of future results.  
Source: [SEC Investor.gov—Build Wealth Over Time](https://www.investor.gov/build-wealth-over-time-through-saving-and-investing)

### Overconfidence

Overconfidence can lead users to believe that they can select winning securities, time markets, interpret live data better than other investors, or take more risk than they can tolerate. Common manifestations include excessive trading, leverage, options, concentrated positions, and treating a short run of success as proof of skill.

The AI should ask the user to state the thesis, downside, time horizon, diversification impact, liquidity need, and exit conditions before discussing a speculative position. It should make clear that access to current price, valuation, beta, holdings, or news data does not create a reliable forecast. Any satellite or speculative allocation should be treated as money the user can afford to lose, not as the core plan for a required goal.

## How an educational AI should guide users

The AI should act as a structured educator, not as a salesperson or an adviser making an unsupported individualized recommendation.

### Recommended interaction

1. **Clarify goals:** amount, priority, flexibility, intended use, and earliest likely withdrawal date.
2. **Map resources and constraints:** income stability, debt, emergency savings, insurance, pensions, dependents, account type, taxes, and concentrated employment assets.
3. **Assess tolerance and capacity separately:** use behavioral scenarios as well as direct questions.
4. **Assess knowledge:** explain unfamiliar terms and products before discussing them; never equate confidence with expertise.
5. **Build goal-specific buckets:** do not let a high-risk retirement portfolio dictate the allocation for a near-term deposit or tuition bill.
6. **Show ranges and trade-offs:** compare conservative, moderate, and growth illustrations; show what a decline could mean in dollars and whether the goal would still be funded.
7. **Use assumptions explicitly:** label return, inflation, contribution, withdrawal, and tax assumptions; avoid presenting projections as promises.
8. **Recommend process over prediction:** diversification, automation, low costs, periodic review, and a written rebalancing rule.
9. **Add behavioral guardrails:** cooling-off periods for large trades, limits on concentrated holdings, no leverage for essential goals, and a rule to review the plan before selling after a decline.
10. **Escalate appropriately:** suggest a qualified financial planner, tax professional, benefits specialist, or attorney when the issue involves complex taxes, debt, pensions, disability, estate planning, business ownership, or a potentially unaffordable loss.

The AI should disclose that this is general educational information, verify current U.S. tax and account rules before stating them, and avoid fabricating contribution limits, expected returns, “safe” allocations, or guarantees. CFP Board’s standards emphasize considering goals, risk tolerance, objectives, and financial and personal circumstances when exercising professional judgment; those same dimensions are a useful safety framework for an educational system.  
Source: [CFP Board—Code and Standards](https://www.cfp.net/ethics/code-of-ethics-and-standards-of-conduct)
