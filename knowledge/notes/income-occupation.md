# income-occupation — Income, occupation, human capital, and workplace benefits

## Core principle: treat earnings as part of the risk picture

A person’s future labor income—often called **human capital**—is an important economic asset. A stable government salary or pension-like career income can make a household’s overall balance sheet more resilient, while a commission-based, cyclical, or single-company career creates more income risk. The financial portfolio should not simply maximize stock exposure because the investor is young or earns a high salary.

Ask:

- How much income is earned, and how much is reliably recurring?
- Could income fall sharply during a recession, illness, injury, strike, deployment, or job change?
- Is income tied to one employer, industry, region, or company stock?
- How portable are the pension, retirement plan, health insurance, and other benefits?
- What debts, dependents, and near-term spending needs compete with investing?
- Does the employer provide a match, pension, stock compensation, disability insurance, life insurance, tuition assistance, or loan repayment?
- Does the person expect to remain in the job long enough to vest in benefits?

General implications:

1. **Higher income increases contribution capacity, not necessarily risk capacity.** A high earner with large debt, dependents, unstable compensation, or a near-term home purchase may need substantial liquidity.
2. **Stable employment can support systematic investing**, but the portfolio should still be diversified.
3. **Variable income requires more cash-flow flexibility.** Contributions can be based on a conservative baseline income, with additional investing after taxes and reserves are funded.
4. **Employer benefits are part of compensation.** Compare total compensation rather than salary alone.
5. **Do not count an unvested or uncertain benefit as guaranteed wealth.** Pension vesting, stock vesting, bonuses, and future promotions should be modeled conservatively.
6. **Protect the ability to earn.** Disability insurance can be particularly important when the household depends heavily on one person’s labor income; disability insurance is designed to replace lost income. CFPB, [Your Money, Your Goals](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/YMYG_Workers_Toolkit_Emp_4-28-16_machine_readable_a.pdf).

## General decision rules

- **If income is low or unstable**, prioritize cash-flow resilience, essential insurance, any available employer match, and eliminating expensive debt before taking substantial market risk.
- **If income is high but debt is also high**, compare the after-tax expected benefit of investing with the guaranteed return from debt repayment; do not assume a high salary makes aggressive investing appropriate.
- **If the employer, industry, and investment portfolio are all concentrated in the same company or sector**, reduce correlated exposure where practical. Diversification spreads money among investments so that one loss does not determine the household’s outcome. Investor.gov, [Diversification](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/diversification).
- **If compensation is irregular**, use a conservative recurring contribution and invest windfalls only after reserving for taxes, operating expenses, and personal cash needs.
- **If a pension is reliable and inflation-adjusted**, it may provide bond-like retirement income; this can justify a somewhat more growth-oriented financial portfolio, but the decision must account for vesting, COLA, survivor benefits, insolvency risk, access to cash, and the possibility of leaving the employer.
- **If a benefit depends on continued employment**, do not treat it as fully earned until vesting and eligibility are clear.

## Doctors and other high-income professionals

### Typical strategic issues

Doctors often have high eventual earning potential but may begin full-time saving late, carry substantial education debt, and face unusually high income-replacement risk. Residents and fellows may have low current income relative to debt and may be eligible for employer retirement plans before becoming attendings.

### Priorities

1. Maintain a workable cash reserve and appropriate health, life, and disability insurance.
2. Make required student-loan payments and determine whether federal loans, income-driven repayment, or Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF) are relevant before refinancing.
3. Capture any employer retirement-plan match.
4. Obtain an individualized disability-insurance evaluation, especially an own-occupation policy where appropriate; review exclusions, residual benefits, inflation protection, future-increase options, and employer coverage limitations.
5. Increase retirement contributions as income rises rather than allowing lifestyle spending to absorb the entire raise.
6. Diversify across tax-advantaged and taxable accounts after considering debt interest rates, tax bracket, liquidity, and planned purchases.
7. Coordinate the portfolio with any pension, practice ownership, real estate, or concentrated position in a medical practice.

Federal Student Aid states that PSLF generally requires qualifying employment and **120 qualifying monthly payments**; qualifying employers include U.S. government entities and certain tax-exempt organizations. Employment and payment rules can change, so borrowers should verify directly with Federal Student Aid before refinancing or changing repayment plans: [PSLF](https://studentaid.gov/manage-loans/forgiveness-cancellation/public-service).

### Resident or fellow

A resident should generally avoid assuming attending-level income before it arrives. Keep near-term cash needs and debt strategy ahead of speculative investing. A Roth IRA may be attractive if taxable compensation and eligibility permit, but contributions should not come from money needed for tuition, loan payments, relocation, or licensing expenses.

### Attending

A large salary does not eliminate the need for an emergency reserve, insurance, or diversification. Automate retirement contributions immediately after a compensation increase. Compare traditional versus Roth contributions using current and expected future marginal tax rates, the expected retirement date, and the availability of employer plans. Consider a taxable account for goals before retirement age or for flexibility beyond plan limits.

## Teachers and public-sector workers

### Pension coordination

A defined-benefit pension can be valuable retirement income, but its economic value depends on:

- Vesting and years-of-service requirements
- Benefit formula and final-average-pay definition
- COLA or inflation protection
- Survivor-option reduction
- Retirement eligibility age
- Portability if the worker changes states or systems
- Health-care benefits
- State or local funding and legal protections
- Interaction with Social Security

A pension is not the same as a liquid investment account. It may reduce the need for bonds in the rest of the portfolio, but workers still need liquid savings for emergencies, career changes, home repairs, health costs, and goals before pension eligibility.

### 403(b) and 457(b)

Teachers, nonprofit employees, and public workers may have access to a **403(b)**, a **governmental 457(b)**, or both. Compare:

- Employer match or nonelective contribution
- Investment menu and expense ratios
- Administrative and contract fees
- Annuity surrender charges
- Roth availability
- Loan and withdrawal provisions
- Rollover rules
- Whether the 457(b) is governmental or nongovernmental

A governmental 457(b) may offer useful access flexibility after separation from service, but plan rules and tax treatment should be verified before taking a distribution. Workers should not select a high-cost annuity merely because it is offered through a school or public employer. Review the actual fee disclosure and investment lineup.

The IRS announced that the **2026 elective-deferral limit is $24,500** for 401(k), 403(b), governmental 457 plans, and the federal Thrift Savings Plan; catch-up rules and limits are separate and should be checked for the applicable year. IRS, [2026 retirement-plan limits](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026-ira-limit-increases-to-7500). Contribution rules must be verified annually.

### Suggested approach

- Treat the pension as one component of retirement income, not as a reason to ignore personal savings.
- Contribute enough to obtain the full match if one exists.
- Favor low-cost, diversified investments where available.
- Coordinate the account allocation with the pension’s inflation and survivor characteristics.
- If expecting to leave public employment, understand vesting and rollover consequences before resigning.
- If PSLF is relevant, preserve qualifying employment and verify payment certification before making irreversible loan decisions.

## Nurses and other health-care workers

Nurses may have relatively stable base wages but variable overtime, shift differentials, travel assignments, bonuses, and employer changes. The work can also involve physical injury, burnout, and disability risk.

Priorities:

1. Base recurring savings on ordinary wages rather than overtime that may disappear.
2. Direct a portion of overtime or bonuses toward reserves, debt reduction, and retirement rather than permanently increasing fixed expenses.
3. Review employer disability, life, health, and retirement benefits; employer-provided disability coverage may be insufficient or nonportable.
4. Capture the retirement-plan match and compare plan fees.
5. Coordinate savings with dependents, childcare, student loans, and shift-related income volatility.
6. Avoid allowing a single hospital system’s stock or local real estate exposure to dominate the household’s wealth.
7. For travel nurses or workers changing employers frequently, maintain an organized rollover and beneficiary process rather than leaving multiple small accounts unmanaged.

A nurse with a pension or public-employer plan should apply the same pension-coordination principles described for teachers. A nurse working for a nonprofit hospital may also need to evaluate whether employment and loans qualify for PSLF rather than assuming that every hospital job qualifies.

## Technology workers with RSUs and ESPP plans

### RSUs

Restricted stock units are generally taxable as compensation when they vest, based on their fair market value under the applicable tax rules. The IRS discusses RSUs and employee stock compensation in [Publication 525](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p525).

The core risk is **correlated exposure**:

- Salary depends on the employer.
- Career progression depends on the employer.
- RSUs and options may depend on the employer’s stock price.
- A recession can reduce both employment prospects and stock value at the same time.

Practical framework:

1. Record vesting dates, shares, tax withholding, blackout periods, and expiration rules.
2. Treat vested shares as a new investment decision, not as permanently “free” money.
3. Establish a written maximum employer-stock exposure based on total net worth and risk capacity.
4. Diversify gradually or at vesting according to the plan and tax circumstances rather than making an emotional decision during a market decline.
5. Reserve cash for taxes; withholding may not cover the person’s final tax liability, especially with high total compensation or multiple employers.
6. Do not let unrealized gains determine the emergency-fund decision.
7. Review ISO, NSO, RSU, and ESPP rules with a tax professional when the amounts are material.

### ESPP

Employee stock purchase plans can provide a discount, but they involve employer-stock concentration, holding-period rules, market risk, and tax complexity. The employee should calculate the value after purchase-price discount, taxes, commissions, and the risk of holding the shares. Avoid using a plan’s discount as justification for accumulating an excessive single-stock position.

### Options and private-company equity

Options may expire worthless, have exercise costs, create tax issues, or be illiquid. Private-company equity should not be treated like cash until liquidity, valuation, vesting, and tax consequences are clear. A worker should not borrow heavily or abandon diversified savings in anticipation of an IPO or acquisition.

## Gig workers and self-employed people

Self-employed income is often volatile and may lack employer health insurance, disability insurance, paid leave, and a workplace retirement match. Separate personal finances from business finances where practical.

### Priorities

1. Maintain a larger operating and personal cash buffer than a worker with highly predictable payroll.
2. Reserve money for federal, state, and self-employment taxes before investing variable receipts.
3. Pay estimated taxes as required and avoid investing money that may be needed for a tax bill.
4. Obtain health, disability, liability, and life insurance appropriate to the business and household.
5. Use a conservative baseline income for recurring contributions; invest additional profits after the tax reserve and operating needs are funded.
6. Consider SEP-IRA, Solo 401(k), SIMPLE IRA, or another plan based on income, employees, desired Roth access, administrative burden, and contribution flexibility.
7. Track business deductions and net earnings carefully because retirement-plan contribution calculations for self-employed individuals use special definitions.

### SEP-IRA

The IRS describes a SEP as allowing contributions based on a percentage of net self-employment earnings, subject to annual limits. A SEP can be simple and useful when contributions need to vary with business income. However:

- Contributions are generally employer contributions, not employee salary deferrals.
- If the business has eligible employees, comparable contributions may be required for them.
- A SEP may be less flexible than a Solo 401(k) for a lower-income sole proprietor seeking employee deferrals or Roth contributions.
- Contributions reduce taxable income but do not remove the need for a separate emergency and tax reserve.

IRS, [Retirement plans for self-employed people](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/retirement-plans-for-self-employed-people).

### Solo 401(k)

A one-participant 401(k), commonly called a Solo 401(k), generally covers a business owner with no employees other than a spouse. The owner can contribute in both employee and employer capacities, subject to the applicable annual limits and special self-employed compensation calculations. Employee elective-deferral limits apply across plans, not independently to every plan a person participates in.

The IRS notes that a one-participant 401(k) must generally file Form 5500-EZ when plan assets reach the applicable filing threshold. Hiring eligible employees can end the simplified one-participant arrangement and trigger additional plan requirements. IRS, [One-participant 401(k) plans](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/one-participant-401k-plans).

## Military members: TSP and BRS

Military members should coordinate the Thrift Savings Plan (TSP) with the **Blended Retirement System (BRS)**, continuation-pay eligibility, service requirements, possible pension benefits, VA benefits, and post-service employment.

For BRS participants, contributing enough to receive the full available government contribution is usually an important priority, provided cash-flow and high-interest-debt needs are addressed. TSP describes automatic and matching contributions and provides current contribution information at [What is a contribution?](https://www.tsp.gov/what-is-contribution/).

Key considerations:

- Understand whether the member is covered by BRS or another retirement system.
- Verify the service’s matching formula, vesting, and eligibility rather than relying on a generic rule.
- Use the TSP’s diversified index-fund choices or an appropriate Lifecycle Fund rather than concentrating in a single sector.
- Coordinate Traditional versus Roth TSP contributions with taxable pay, tax-exempt combat-zone pay, state residence, and expected future tax rates.
- Do not sacrifice essential cash reserves or high-interest-debt repayment solely to maximize contributions.
- Review beneficiaries after marriage, divorce, childbirth, and deployment.
- Treat a future military pension as conditional on service and eligibility; do not count it as fully secured if separation is uncertain.
- When leaving service, compare leaving assets in the TSP with an IRA or new employer plan based on fees, investment choices, creditor protection, tax treatment, and administrative simplicity.

The IRS’s **2026 elective-deferral limit is $24,500** for the TSP, with separate catch-up provisions for eligible ages. IRS, [2026 retirement-plan limits](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026). Limits and BRS rules should be verified annually.

## Students

Students usually have high potential future human capital but limited current cash flow and low short-term risk capacity. A long theoretical investment horizon does not justify investing money needed for tuition, rent, books, transportation, health expenses, or an imminent move.

Priorities:

1. Avoid investing borrowed money or money needed for education and living costs.
2. Build a modest liquid reserve appropriate to the student’s support system and expenses.
3. Pay attention to high-interest debt and preserve eligibility for necessary financial aid.
4. If employed, consider an employer match and a Roth IRA when the student has taxable earned compensation and meets eligibility rules.
5. Use a low-cost, diversified investment rather than attempting to select individual stocks.
6. Develop savings and tax habits before increasing portfolio complexity.
7. If parents or relatives are funding education, coordinate a 529 or other education account with the expected education timeline rather than placing tuition money in volatile assets.

A Roth IRA contribution generally requires taxable compensation; scholarships, gifts, loans, and investment income do not automatically create Roth contribution room. Verify the current IRS rules and income limits before contributing. The IRS lists the **2026 IRA contribution limit as $7,500**, subject to eligibility and income rules: [2026 IRA limits](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026-ira-limit-increases-to-7500).

For a student with earned income, a small, automatic contribution can be valuable for building behavior, but it should not come at the cost of unmet tuition, housing, tax, or emergency needs. A student with no earned income may use a taxable savings account or other appropriate cash vehicle instead of attempting to fund a retirement account.

## How occupation should affect asset allocation

Occupation should influence the portfolio through **income risk**, not through stereotypes.

- **Stable, diversified public-sector income plus a pension:** The household may have more capacity for growth assets in the investment portfolio, but needs liquid savings outside the pension.
- **High-income but debt-heavy professional:** Preserve liquidity and insurance; consider a balanced debt-versus-investing plan instead of immediately maximizing risk.
- **Cyclical technology or finance worker:** Avoid combining employer stock, career risk, and a highly aggressive portfolio.
- **Variable gig or commission worker:** Hold more liquidity and use flexible contribution rules; do not treat a strong month as permanent income.
- **Physically demanding worker:** Give greater weight to disability coverage and the possibility of earlier retirement or career transition.
- **Student or new graduate:** Protect near-term education and relocation funds; invest only money with a genuinely long horizon.
- **Worker with a strong pension:** Evaluate the pension’s reliability, inflation protection, and liquidity before reducing bonds elsewhere.

These are starting points, not fixed allocation formulas. The final mix should be driven by each goal’s date, ability to withstand a loss, need for liquidity, tax situation, insurance coverage, debt obligations, and willingness to remain invested during a downturn.

## Questions the investing assistant should ask

1. What is your occupation and employment type: salaried, hourly, commission, contract, self-employed, military, student, or retired?
2. What portion of your income is guaranteed, and what portion comes from overtime, bonuses, commissions, RSUs, ESPP shares, or gig work?
3. How long do you expect to remain with the employer?
4. Do you have a pension? What are its vesting, retirement-age, COLA, survivor, and portability provisions?
5. Which plans are available: 401(k), 403(b), governmental 457(b), TSP, SEP-IRA, Solo 401(k), SIMPLE IRA, HSA, or a defined-benefit pension?
6. What is the employer match or contribution, and what fees and investment options does the plan provide?
7. Do you hold employer stock, options, RSUs, or ESPP shares? What percentage of total assets and future compensation do they represent?
8. Are student loans eligible for PSLF or another forgiveness program?
9. What disability, life, health, and liability insurance is provided by work, and is it portable?
10. Are income and expenses likely to change because of residency, deployment, job transition, business seasonality, or graduation?
11. Which money must remain available for tuition, taxes, business expenses, a home purchase, dependents, or a career transition?
12. What are the pension and retirement-plan beneficiary designations, and when were they last reviewed?
