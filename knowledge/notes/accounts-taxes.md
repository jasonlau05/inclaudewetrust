## `accounts-taxes`

### Core principle

Choose the account based on **tax rate, employer benefits, health coverage, time horizon, liquidity needs, and the purpose of the money**. Account selection and investment selection are separate decisions: a diversified, appropriately risked portfolio can generally be held inside most of these accounts.

The following figures are for **tax year 2026**, unless otherwise stated. Federal rules do not determine every plan feature; employer documents, state law, and the taxpayer’s modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) also matter.

## 2026 contribution and income limits

| Account | 2026 IRS limit or income rule | When it is generally considered |
|---|---|---|
| **401(k)** | Employee elective-deferral limit: **$24,500**. Age-50-and-older catch-up: **$8,000**. For ages **60–63**, the higher catch-up limit is **$11,250**. Traditional and Roth 401(k) contributions share the same employee limit. [IRS 2026 retirement limits](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026-ira-limit-increases-to-7500) | Usually a first-line retirement account when the employer offers a match, especially after establishing basic cash reserves and addressing high-interest debt. |
| **403(b)** | Generally the same **$24,500** employee limit and catch-up limits as a 401(k). Some plans permit an additional plan-specific **15-year-service catch-up**; eligibility and the amount depend on the plan and service history. [IRS Notice 2025-67](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-25-67.pdf) | Common for teachers, employees of public schools, colleges, hospitals, churches, and nonprofits. Compare investment expenses carefully because some 403(b) contracts have high-cost annuities. |
| **Governmental 457(b)** | Employee deferral limit: **$24,500** in 2026, with the same age-based catch-up limits described above. A special final-three-years catch-up may apply instead of the age-based catch-up, subject to plan rules and unused prior-year limits. A 457(b) limit is generally separate from the 401(k)/403(b) limit. [IRS 2026 retirement limits](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026-ira-limit-increases-to-7500); [IRS Notice 2025-67](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-25-67.pdf) | Particularly valuable for government employees who may retire or change careers before traditional retirement age. Distributions from a governmental 457(b) generally are not subject to the additional federal early-distribution tax that usually applies to other qualified-plan distributions. Verify plan and distribution rules. |
| **Non-governmental 457(b)** | Generally uses the same statutory deferral framework, but plan design and creditor treatment differ. [IRS 457(b) guidance](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/retirement-plans-for-governmental-and-nongovernmental-employers/section-457b-deferred-compensation-plans) | Treat as an employer promise rather than the same type of protected trust used by many governmental plans. Review employer-creditor risk, distribution restrictions, and the plan document before prioritizing it. |
| **Traditional IRA** | Total traditional and Roth IRA contributions combined: **$7,500**; age-50-and-older IRA catch-up: **$1,100**. There is generally no income limit to make a traditional IRA contribution if the taxpayer has sufficient taxable compensation, but the deduction can be reduced or eliminated based on workplace-plan coverage and MAGI. [IRS 2026 retirement limits](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026-ira-limit-increases-to-7500) | Consider when the contribution is deductible, when current tax relief is valuable, or when tax-deferred retirement savings are needed. A nondeductible traditional IRA may be less attractive unless used as part of a carefully planned Roth conversion strategy. |
| **Roth IRA** | Combined traditional/Roth IRA contribution limit: **$7,500**, plus the **$1,100** age-based catch-up. Direct Roth IRA contributions phase out at 2026 MAGI of **$153,000–$168,000** for single filers and heads of household, **$242,000–$252,000** for married filing jointly, and **$0–$10,000** for married filing separately. [IRS 2026 retirement limits](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026-ira-limit-increases-to-7500) | Often attractive for people who expect a higher future tax rate, younger workers, people with many years for tax-free compounding, and people who value tax-free qualified retirement withdrawals and no lifetime required minimum distributions for the original owner. |
| **Roth 401(k)** | No income limit to participate. Employee Roth and traditional 401(k) deferrals share the **$24,500** 2026 employee limit, plus applicable catch-up limits. [IRS Roth comparison chart](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/roth-comparison-chart); [IRS 2026 retirement limits](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026-ira-limit-increases-to-7500) | Useful for high earners who cannot contribute directly to a Roth IRA, or for workers who want tax diversification. Contributions are after-tax; qualified distributions are generally tax-free. The employer plan may have higher fees or fewer investment choices than an IRA. |
| **HSA** | 2026 contribution limit, including employer contributions: **$4,400** for self-only coverage and **$8,750** for family coverage. The age-55-and-older catch-up is **$1,000** per eligible individual. A qualifying HDHP must generally have a minimum deductible of **$1,700** self-only or **$3,400** family, with an annual out-of-pocket maximum no higher than **$8,500** self-only or **$17,000** family. [IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-19](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-25-19.pdf); [IRS Publication 969](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p969) | Often a high priority for someone enrolled in an HSA-eligible HDHP who can afford the deductible. Contributions can be deductible or excluded from income, investment growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. Do not sacrifice adequate emergency cash merely to maximize an HSA. Medicare enrollment, other coverage, or dependent status can affect eligibility. |
| **529 plan** | There is no single federal annual contribution limit or federal income limit comparable to an IRA. Each state plan sets an aggregate account limit, and contributions generally cannot exceed what is reasonably needed for the beneficiary’s qualified education expenses. Large contributions can be subject to gift-tax rules; state tax deductions or credits vary. [IRS 529 Q&A](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/529-plans-questions-and-answers); [IRS Publication 970](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p970) | Consider for a likely education expense when the investor wants tax-free growth and tax-free withdrawals for qualified education expenses. Check the state’s plan costs, investment menu, state tax benefit, beneficiary-change rules, and the possibility that the student may not use the funds for education. |
| **Taxable brokerage** | No IRS contribution limit or income ceiling. Interest, dividends, and realized capital gains are generally reportable; losses may have tax value subject to tax rules. [IRS Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550) | Appropriate for goals before retirement age, a house or other medium-term goal when investments are suitable for the time horizon, early-retirement bridge assets, and savings after tax-advantaged accounts are filled. It offers liquidity without retirement-account withdrawal rules, but has less tax shelter. |
| **Custodial UGMA/UTMA account** | No IRA-style federal annual contribution limit or income ceiling, but contributions are irrevocable gifts to the child. Gift-tax, investment-income, and “kiddie tax” rules can apply. [IRS Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550); [IRS Publication 929](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p929) | Consider when the money is intended to become the child’s property, not merely to fund a parent-controlled goal. The child generally receives control under the applicable state’s termination rules. The assets can affect need-based aid and may be used by the child for any lawful purpose after control transfers. |
| **Custodial Roth IRA for a teen** | The teen must have taxable compensation. The 2026 contribution is the lesser of **$7,500** or the teen’s taxable compensation for the year. Roth IRA income phaseouts still apply, although most working teens are below them. [IRS 2026 retirement limits](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026-ira-limit-increases-to-7500); [IRS Publication 590-A](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590a) | One of the strongest long-term options for a minor with legitimate earned income. A parent or grandparent may provide the cash for the contribution, but the teen must actually have sufficient earned income. The account is custodial until the applicable age under state law, and the investment horizon is unusually long. |

## Roth versus traditional: practical tax-bracket heuristics

The relevant comparison is the **marginal federal tax rate on the next dollar**, not the taxpayer’s average tax rate. State taxes, deductions, credits, employer matching, expected retirement income, and future tax-law uncertainty can change the answer.

For **2026**, the principal federal marginal-bracket thresholds are:

- **Single filers:** 10% through **$12,400**; 12% through **$50,400**; 22% through **$105,700**; 24% through **$201,775**; 32% through **$256,225**; 35% through **$640,600**; and 37% above that level.
- **Married filing jointly:** 10% through **$24,800**; 12% through **$100,800**; 22% through **$211,400**; 24% through **$403,550**; 32% through **$512,450**; 35% through **$768,700**; and 37% above that level.

These are taxable-income thresholds, not salary thresholds. [IRS 2026 tax inflation adjustments](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-releases-tax-inflation-adjustments-for-tax-year-2026-including-amendments-from-the-one-big-beautiful-bill)

### General heuristics

- **10% or 12% bracket:** Roth contributions are often attractive because the current tax cost is relatively low and the investor may earn substantially more later. Traditional contributions can still make sense during an unusually low-income year, when deductions or credits are especially valuable, or when retirement income is expected to be very low.
- **22% or 24% bracket:** This is often a “split” range. Traditional contributions may be preferable if the current marginal rate is clearly higher than the expected retirement rate; Roth contributions can provide tax diversification and may be useful if future income, pensions, required distributions, or tax rates could be high.
- **32%, 35%, or 37% bracket:** Traditional workplace contributions often provide more immediate tax value, particularly when the taxpayer expects to occupy lower brackets in retirement. Roth contributions can still be sensible for tax diversification, anticipated high retirement income, estate planning, or a taxpayer who expects tax rates or income to remain high.
- **Low-income or transition year:** A Roth contribution or partial Roth conversion may be attractive when current taxable income is temporarily low. Conversions should be modeled carefully because they can affect tax brackets, credits, Medicare premiums, and other income-based items.
- **High-income Roth strategy:** A workplace Roth 401(k) has no income limit, but a direct Roth IRA contribution may be restricted by the MAGI phaseouts above. A “backdoor Roth” strategy can create taxable consequences under the pro-rata rule if the taxpayer has pre-tax traditional, SEP, or SIMPLE IRA balances; review this with a qualified tax professional.
- **Traditional IRA deduction:** A traditional IRA contribution is not automatically deductible. For 2026, when the contributor is covered by a workplace retirement plan, the deduction phases out at **$81,000–$91,000** MAGI for a single filer and **$129,000–$149,000** for married filing jointly when the contributing spouse is covered. When the contributor is not covered but is married to someone who is covered, the joint-filer phaseout is **$242,000–$252,000**. [IRS 2026 retirement limits](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026-ira-limit-increases-to-7500)

### Tax diversification

A mixture of traditional, Roth, HSA, and taxable assets can provide flexibility in retirement. For example, taxable assets may fund spending without an IRA distribution, traditional accounts may fill lower tax brackets, and Roth assets may be reserved for late retirement, large one-time expenses, or heirs. The best mix depends on the household’s projected income and tax situation rather than a universal percentage.

## Account-selection sequence

A general educational sequence is:

1. Keep adequate emergency cash and address high-interest debt.
2. Contribute enough to a 401(k), 403(b), or governmental 457(b) to receive the full employer match.
3. If eligible and able to fund the deductible, consider maximizing an HSA.
4. Use a Roth IRA or deductible traditional IRA according to MAGI, tax bracket, and future-tax expectations.
5. Increase workplace-plan contributions, using traditional, Roth, or a combination.
6. Use a 529 for a defined education goal after balancing education funding against the investor’s own retirement security.
7. Use taxable brokerage accounts for flexible, non-retirement goals and for early-retirement liquidity.
8. Use custodial accounts only when the investor intentionally wants to make an irrevocable gift to the child.

This is not a mandatory order. A public employee with a pension, a person pursuing early retirement, someone seeking PSLF, or a household with imminent education or housing expenses may reasonably use a different sequence.

## Special cautions

- Employer matching rules, vesting, plan fees, investment choices, loan provisions, and distribution rules can be more important than the account label.
- A Roth contribution is not automatically better merely because withdrawals may be tax-free; the investor pays the tax upfront.
- A traditional contribution is not automatically better merely because it produces a current deduction; future withdrawals and required distributions may be taxable.
- Do not use a 529 or custodial account for a goal that may require unrestricted access to the money unless the ownership and withdrawal consequences are understood.
- Do not assume a high-deductible health plan is appropriate solely because an HSA is available; compare premiums, deductible, out-of-pocket maximum, expected medical spending, and cash reserves.
- Custodial Roth IRA contributions require real earned income and accurate records. Cash gifts from a parent can fund the contribution, but invented or inflated compensation is not acceptable.
- Tax treatment can differ by state, particularly for 529 contributions, municipal bonds, retirement deductions, and Roth conversions.

## Yearly verification note

**Verify every limit before making or changing contributions.** IRS thresholds, catch-up provisions, tax brackets, HSA eligibility rules, retirement-plan regulations, state 529 benefits, and employer-plan terms can change each year. For each tax year, check the latest IRS cost-of-living-adjustment notice, the applicable IRS publications, the employer’s current Summary Plan Description, and state tax guidance. Confirm filing status, MAGI, taxable compensation, workplace-plan coverage, HSA eligibility, and year-to-date contributions before relying on a limit. This material is general education, not tax, legal, or investment advice.
